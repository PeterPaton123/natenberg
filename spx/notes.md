# Volatility skew notes

Source: [Dawn of a New Era Brings on the Existence of Skew](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/dawn-of-a-new-era-brings-on-the-existence-of-skew/)

1. The extent to which market demand for downside protection exceeds upside exposure is emblematic of the perception of outlier (left tail) events. Understanding the supply and demand dynamics is crucial to understanding skew.
2. Many market participants are passively long through pensions, investments, and savings. For these long equity holdings, their market-direction risk is to the downside. There is much more concern about a 2% down day than a 2% up day. This creates substantial asymmetric demand for downside protection (puts). Market participants are also willing to finance the cost of that protection by selling calls, giving up some potential upside.
3. Market makers and dealers (banks), the primary suppliers of downside protection, are especially cognizant of the fact that markets tend to move down with greater velocity than when they move higher. They also know that there is unequal, or skewed, demand for OTM puts and supply of OTM calls.
4. If option prices across strikes are generated using one constant volatility in Black-Scholes, their implied volatility curve is flat for a given expiry. In reality, willingness to pay for downside protection and upside speculation can raise the prices of tail options relative to that baseline, producing a volatility smile. Asymmetric demand and supply, particularly stronger demand for downside protection, contribute to the skew seen in SPX options.
5. Cboe introduced the Cboe SKEW Index as a benchmark for the downside tail risk priced into SPX options. It generally rises as the implied volatility curve becomes more steeply skewed toward downside puts. Although Cboe describes it as a global measure of the curve's slope, its calculation uses the option-implied skewness of 30-day returns, rather than directly calculating an IV slope. See the [SKEW white paper](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/documents/SKEWwhitepaperjan2011.pdf).
6. A steeper downside skew generally means downside protection is priced more richly relative to upside options, consistent with stronger demand to hedge. However, it can also reflect sellers requiring more compensation for crash risk or fewer sellers supplying protection. Skew therefore reveals the relative price of downside risk, rather than uniquely measuring buyers' appetite to hedge.
7. The October 1987 crash sensitized the market to the possibility of large downward jumps in the S&P 500. The distribution of S&P 500 log returns is unlikely to be normal if there are large jumps in returns. Jumps fatten the tails, and asymmetric jumps skew the distribution.
